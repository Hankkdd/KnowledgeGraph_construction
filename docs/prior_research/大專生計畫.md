\begin{abstract}
\normalfont
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has become a common approach for portfolio allocation because it can optimize sequential trading decisions directly from market observations. Existing DRL trading systems, however, usually construct their states from price-volume features and technical indicators, leaving company-level business relationships outside the model state. This paper studies an LLM-augmented knowledge graph framework for reinforcement learning in stock trading. The framework constructs a financial-report knowledge graph with GraphRAG, converts company-level relational structure into PyKEEN embeddings, and uses these embeddings to build an attention-based state encoder for fixed-portfolio allocation. The investment universe is the Dow 30, while the allocation target is a fixed five-stock portfolio consisting of AAPL, NVDA, AMZN, IBM, and MSFT. The attention encoder uses the fixed five companies as queries, Dow 30 company embeddings as keys, and Dow 30 OHLCV windows as values. The encoder is pretrained with next-day return supervision and then frozen during reinforcement learning so that the allocation stage only trains a lightweight portfolio head. This two-stage design separates representation learning from policy optimization and allows the agent to use financial-report relational knowledge without directly injecting predicted returns into the action logits. The empirical protocol compares three core allocation variants: equal weight, a uniform-attention ablation based on identical company embeddings, and the proposed KG-attention RL model. MVO and the DJIA are included as external benchmarks, while additional ablations examine representation freezing, cash allocation, alternative graph structure, and KG corruption.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Portfolio allocation is a sequential decision problem in which an agent repeatedly adjusts asset weights under changing market conditions. Deep reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for this setting because it can optimize trading actions directly from historical observations. Most DRL trading systems, however, construct their states mainly from price-volume features and technical indicators. Such states describe how an asset has moved, but they provide limited information about why firms may move together or transmit risk to one another. In equity markets, companies are connected through supply chains, product competition, shared technologies, regulatory exposure, and sector-level shocks. These relationships are often described in financial reports but are not directly represented in standard OHLCV-based trading states.

This gap matters for portfolio allocation. A price-only agent may observe that two stocks are correlated, but it does not know whether the correlation comes from direct competition, supplier dependence, shared customers, or exposure to the same macro risk. These relations affect how shocks propagate across firms and how diversification should be interpreted. A portfolio model that treats all assets as independent price series may therefore miss information that is relevant to risk control and rebalancing decisions.

Knowledge graphs (KGs) provide one way to represent this missing structure. A KG organizes entities and relations as machine-readable triples, making it possible to encode companies, business concepts, risk factors, and inter-company links in a structured form. In this study, financial reports are processed with GraphRAG to construct a report-derived KG. The resulting graph is then converted into PyKEEN company embeddings, which serve as structural representations of the Dow 30 companies.

In this study, the extracted KG embeddings are incorporated into a fixed-portfolio attention architecture. The fixed five portfolio companies serve as queries, all Dow 30 company embeddings serve as keys, and rolling Dow 30 OHLCV windows serve as values. The attention output is a $5 \times 128$ state representation consumed by a lightweight allocation head. The attention modules are pretrained with next-day return supervision and then frozen during the RL stage, so that the allocation stage focuses on portfolio weights rather than jointly relearning the entire representation.

A central design concern in this framework is that not every edge extracted or projected by an LLM-based pipeline is reliable or economically meaningful: two companies can appear similar simply because their reports share accounting vocabulary, and LLM-based extraction can misread domain terminology or assert relations unsupported by the source text. Because of this, the proposed framework does not treat the generated KG as automatically trustworthy. Instead, KG construction (Section~III-C) is paired with diagnostic checks that distinguish direct company-company evidence from projected, concept-mediated edges and evaluate whether the resulting structure carries economically meaningful information before it is used as a state input to the trading agent (Section~III-D). This does not eliminate extraction error, but it makes the reliability of the KG an object of evaluation rather than an assumption, and residual risk is discussed as a limitation in Section~VI.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\raggedright
\item We formulate a financial-report KG pipeline for fixed-portfolio reinforcement learning, connecting GraphRAG-based report indexing, PyKEEN embeddings, and allocation-oriented policy optimization, with diagnostics that distinguish direct company-company evidence from projected, concept-mediated edges.
\item We propose a KG-to-market attention state encoder that uses fixed portfolio companies as queries, Dow 30 company embeddings as keys, and Dow 30 OHLCV windows as values.
\item We adopt a two-stage training design that first pretrains the attention representation with return-prediction and allocation warm-start losses, then freezes the encoder during portfolio policy optimization, keeping the expensive LLM-based KG construction off the online decision path.
\item We define an experimental protocol comprising equal weight, a uniform-attention ablation, the proposed KG-attention RL model, external benchmarks, and targeted ablations designed to assess the incremental value of KG-derived relational structure.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning on Trading}
Reinforcement learning formalizes sequential decision-making as an agent that observes a state, selects an action, and receives a reward that it seeks to maximize over time \cite{sutton2018}. Trading is a natural application of this formulation because portfolio weights can be treated as actions, realized returns can be treated as rewards, and market observations can be treated as states, without requiring an explicit model of future price dynamics. Reinforcement learning has long been used to model trading as a sequential decision problem. Early work such as Moody and Saffell applied RL to trading rules and portfolio decisions, showing that reward-driven agents can be trained directly on financial objectives rather than on one-step price prediction \cite{moody1998}. Deep RL later expanded this direction by replacing hand-designed state discretization with neural representations. Deng et al. combined deep neural architectures with reinforcement learning for financial signal representation and trading, allowing agents to learn nonlinear price patterns from historical market data \cite{deng2017}. These methods established the feasibility of DRL-based trading, but they remained heavily dependent on price-derived states.

Subsequent research attempted to improve market states by adding non-price information. Avramelou et al. incorporated multimodal features into DRL trading systems, and Kirtac and Germano used LLM-derived sentiment indicators in PPO-based portfolio allocation \cite{avramelou2024,kirtac2025}. These studies show that textual or semantic signals can improve trading decisions when price series alone are insufficient. However, sentiment-based features usually represent short-term market reactions. They do not explicitly model persistent firm-to-firm structure such as supply-chain dependence, competition, or common business risks.

Another line of work extends trading agents into multi-agent settings. Kumlungmak and Vateekul proposed a MAPPO-based multi-agent system for cryptocurrency trading, capturing interactions among multiple agents in a shared environment \cite{kumlungmak2023}. Multi-agent designs can represent richer market behavior, but they increase computational cost and still require a clear representation of the market state. If the state is built mainly from price movement, the agent remains limited by the same lack of explicit business structure.

In summary, RL-based trading research has evolved from tabular methods to deep neural policies and from price-only states to multimodal market representations. The remaining limitation is that most models treat non-price information as another feature stream rather than as a persistent relational structure among firms. This motivates the present study to represent financial-report-derived company relationships as a KG-based state component for portfolio allocation.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph on Trading}
A knowledge graph represents entities and relations in a structured graph, and KG embedding methods project this symbolic structure into continuous vector spaces for downstream learning \cite{ji2022,peng2023,wang2017,nickel2016}. Automatic KG construction and KG completion further support the extraction and densification of graph structure from raw text and heterogeneous data sources \cite{zhong2023,chen2020}. These techniques are relevant to finance because company relationships are often described in documents rather than stored as clean numerical variables.

Financial KGs have been used to model industrial ecosystems, event relations, and report-level financial knowledge. Cheng et al. used KG-based event embeddings for quantitative investment, while Zehra et al. constructed a financial-report query system based on financial KG representations \cite{cheng2020,zehra2021}. More recent work has focused on financial causality KGs and core financial KGs for market analysis and return prediction \cite{xu2024,fu2018,kertkeidkachorn2023}. These studies demonstrate that graph structure can represent business relations that are difficult to infer from prices alone. However, many of them use the KG for prediction, explanation, or retrieval rather than as a state representation for reinforcement learning in portfolio allocation.

A related line of work applies graph neural networks (GNNs) directly to financial KGs to propagate information across connected companies. Mozumder et al. combined financial KGs with GNNs to bridge traditional finance and blockchain ecosystems, using message passing over company and entity nodes to produce relational representations for downstream analysis \cite{mozumder2025}. This line of work is architecturally close to the present study in that both use a graph-structured encoder over company-level entities, and both use PyKEEN-style or GNN-style embeddings as inputs to a downstream model. The two approaches differ in how the graph representation is consumed: GNN-based financial analysis frameworks typically propagate information through explicit message passing along graph edges at inference time, whereas the present study precomputes static KG embeddings offline (Section~III-C) and consumes them through an attention mechanism over a fixed query set at each trading date (Section~III-E). This choice is one instance of the broader offline/online separation discussed in Section~III-H, and it means the relational computation itself does not need to be repeated at every trading step, unlike GNN architectures that recompute message passing over the graph for each forward pass.

Dynamic financial KGs address part of this limitation by updating graph structure over time. Li and Sanna Passino proposed FinDKG, which uses LLMs to build dynamic KGs from global financial news, and Miao et al. studied dynamic financial KG construction with reinforcement learning and transfer learning \cite{li2024,miao2019}. These approaches are useful for tracking event-driven market changes, but news-based dynamic graphs may also introduce short-lived noise. Financial reports provide a different source of information: they are lower frequency, but they contain persistent disclosures about business structure, risk factors, competition, and strategic dependencies.

This study uses GraphRAG-derived financial-report KGs as structural context for allocation-oriented RL. This does not claim to solve the full dynamic KG problem: it uses a static KG for architecture experiments, while fully point-in-time KG snapshots for leakage-free historical backtesting are treated as a scope boundary rather than a solved problem here.

\subsection{LLM-Based Extraction Reliability and Downstream Risk}
A separate line of concern, distinct from what a KG represents, is how reliably it can be extracted from text in the first place. LLM-based entity and relation extraction inherits the general reliability limitations of large language models: outputs can be fluent but unsupported by the source document, and long, jargon-dense inputs such as SEC 10-K filings increase the chance that a model conflates similarly named entities or misinterprets domain-specific financial terminology \cite{ji2022,zhong2023}. Automatic KG construction surveys have identified extraction noise and entity ambiguity as persistent challenges even before any downstream embedding or prediction step is applied \cite{zhong2023,chen2020}. In a financial setting, this risk is amplified: incorrect relations can propagate past graph quality into the embedding space used as a reinforcement-learning state, and from there into allocation decisions.

This paper does not adopt a single guaranteed fix for extraction hallucination. It follows a defense-in-depth strategy consistent with the KG construction literature: syntactic and lexical filtering of untyped and boilerplate rows at the triple-building stage (Section~III-C), diagnostic reporting of what was filtered and why (Section~III-D), manual sampling of extracted and projected edges to estimate precision against the source reports (Section~III-D), and a controlled backtesting stress test that injects edge removal, relation shuffling, and false-edge corruption into the KG to measure how sensitive downstream allocation performance is to this class of extraction error (Section~IV-C, item 10). A further option is to sample multiple extraction passes and retain only relations that are consistent across samples, trading additional inference cost for reduced variance in the extracted triples; the present framework does not include this self-consistency voting step and relies instead on rule-based filtering and manual auditing, a scope limitation discussed in Section~VI.

\subsection{Positioning of This Study}
Relative to the RL-on-trading literature reviewed in Section~II-A, this study replaces a price-only or sentiment-augmented state with a KG-derived structural state, while keeping the allocation-only formulation deliberately narrow so that the effect of the KG component can be isolated. Relative to the financial-KG literature reviewed in Section~II-B, this study differs in using the KG as an RL state input rather than as a standalone prediction, explanation, or retrieval target, and in treating KG quality as something to be diagnosed rather than assumed. The two main risks are that projected or similarity-based edges may not carry economic meaning and that LLM-based extraction errors may propagate into downstream allocation decisions. The experimental design evaluates these risks through separate direct-versus-projected edge diagnostics, KG perturbation tests, and non-KG controls. The frozen two-stage design limits policy-stage adaptation of the representation, but it does not itself correct errors introduced during KG construction.

\section{Methodology}

Throughout this paper, ``reinforcement learning'' and ``RL'' denote the sequential-decision formulation of Section~II-A, in which an agent observes a market state, takes an allocation action, and is optimized against a reward defined on realized portfolio growth. Within this formulation, the allocation head is trained by a direct/differentiable policy-gradient method (Section~III-G): gradients of realized log portfolio growth are backpropagated directly through the policy network via the differentiable trading environment, with no value function, no advantage estimate, and no model-free sampling of the kind used by DQN or actor-critic algorithms such as PPO.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
This study focuses on allocation-only portfolio management rather than stock selection. Let the trading universe be a set of $N=30$ Dow component stocks, denoted as $\mathcal{U}=\{1,\ldots,N\}$. The portfolio allocation set is fixed as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathrm{AAPL}, \mathrm{NVDA}, \mathrm{AMZN}, \mathrm{IBM}, \mathrm{MSFT}\},
\end{equation}
where $|\mathcal{P}|=M=5$. The main configuration allocates capital among the fixed five stocks without a cash asset. At each trading date $t$, the allocation head produces a raw target-weight vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_t
=
\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t
=
[w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{1,t},\ldots,w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{M,t}],
\end{equation}
where the softmax allocation head enforces the no-short-selling and budget constraints
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{M} w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,t}=1,
\qquad
w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,t}\geq 0.
\end{equation}
The raw target weights are subsequently transformed by the concentration-cap and action-blending operations defined below to obtain the executable target weights $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_t$. In the cash-enabled ablation, the action vector is extended with a cash component $w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{0,t}$ and normalized over the resulting $M+1$ assets.

The stock logits $\mathbf{z}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ produced by the allocation head (Section~III-E) are converted to $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t$ through temperature-scaled softmax,
\begin{equation}
w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,t}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_t/\tau)_i,
\end{equation}
where $\tau>0$ is the action temperature (division, not multiplication): larger $\tau$ flattens the allocation toward uniform weights, while $\tau\to 0^+$ approaches a hard arg-max allocation. The main configuration uses $\tau=2.0$ (Table~\ref{tab:config}).

In the main configuration, the raw stock weights are passed through a post-softmax concentration-cap operator $\Pi_{w_{\max}}$, with $w_{\max}\in[1/M,1)$. This cap is not a property of the softmax function itself; applying $\Pi_{w_{\max}}$ to $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t$ (summing to 1 over the $M$ stocks) produces the capped target weights $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{cap}}_t=\Pi_{w_{\max}}(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t)$. The operator (defined constructively in Appendix~A) redistributes any mass above $w_{\max}$ to the remaining entries in proportion to their room below the cap, and is chosen to satisfy two properties for any input simplex point:
\begin{equation}
w^{\mathrm{cap}}_{i,t}\in[0,w_{\max}] \ \ \forall i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{M} w^{\mathrm{cap}}_{i,t}=1,
\end{equation}
i.e., $\Pi_{w_{\max}}$ maps a feasible portfolio to another feasible, cap-respecting portfolio rather than merely clipping and rescaling (Appendix~A shows why a naive clip-then-rescale operator does not have this property). Since the previous executed weights $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_{t-1}$ are cap-respecting by the same argument at the prior step (and the initial equal-weight allocation satisfies the cap because $w_{\max}\geq 1/M$), the blending step below is a convex combination of two feasible, cap-respecting portfolios, and is therefore itself feasible and cap-respecting without needing to reapply $\Pi_{w_{\max}}$ after blending. To stabilize daily rebalancing, the capped allocation is blended with the previous portfolio weights:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_t
=
(1-\lambda)\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_{t-1}
+
\lambda \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{cap}}_t,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the action blending coefficient. The main configuration uses $\lambda=0.025$, which makes daily portfolio changes gradual and reduces turnover mechanically. Therefore, turnover should be interpreted jointly with the action-smoothing design rather than as a pure outcome of the learned reward.

\subsection{Data Sources and Preprocessing}
The framework uses two categories of data: market data and financial-report text. The market data consist of daily open, high, low, close, and volume (OHLCV) observations for the Dow 30 universe. Before being assembled into windows, every raw feature is converted into a log-return relative to the previous trading day rather than used as a raw price or volume level:
\begin{equation}
x_{i,t}^{(f)}=\log\!\left(\frac{p_{i,t}^{(f)}}{p_{i,t-1}^{(f)}}\right), \quad f\in\{\text{open, high, low, close, volume}\},
\end{equation}
where the ratio is undefined at the first available date and is filled with 0 in that case. This transform is applied identically to all five channels, including volume, and no further cross-sectional standardization (e.g., z-scoring across the Dow 30 universe at each date) is applied on top of it. The transform serves two purposes: it removes the price- and volume-scale differences across the 30 companies (a company trading near \$200 and one trading near \$20 would otherwise contribute very differently to the flattened value projection $f_v$ of Section~III-E), and it converts every OHLCV channel into approximately stationary, comparable units before the rolling window is formed. For each company $i$ at trading date $t$, a rolling window of length $L=30$ over these log-return features is used:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{i,t-L+1:t} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times F}, \quad F=5.
\end{equation}
Stacking all companies gives the market context tensor:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times L \times F}=\mathbb{R}^{30 \times 30 \times 5}.
\end{equation}

The financial-report text is used to construct the KG. SEC financial reports, obtained from the SEC EDGAR system \cite{secedgar}, are treated as long-form disclosures that contain management discussion, risk factors, business dependencies, competition, strategic relationships, and other non-numeric information. These disclosures are relatively low frequency but can represent persistent structural relationships among firms, so the financial-report KG is used as a structural context source rather than as a high-frequency sentiment signal.

\subsection{GraphRAG-Based Financial Knowledge Graph Construction}
The GraphRAG pipeline \cite{edge2024graphrag} converts financial-report text into graph artifacts. Starting from raw reports, the setup workflow checks report availability, retrieves market data, prepares GraphRAG input, and then runs GraphRAG indexing externally to obtain entity and relationship tables. The downstream pipeline validates the GraphRAG output, aligns company names with ticker symbols, builds PyKEEN triples, trains the KG embedding model, exports company embeddings, checks the KG tensor, and produces diagnostic files for GraphRAG usefulness.

The ticker graph combines two kinds of relationship evidence. First, direct company-to-company edges are retained when GraphRAG extracts explicit relations between companies. Second, projected edges are constructed when two companies share meaningful business concepts, risk exposures, or report-derived entities. To reduce noise, the triple-building stage filters untyped \texttt{related} rows, generic metric rows, and SEC/accounting boilerplate concepts before PyKEEN training. The rejected rows are preserved as diagnostic output so that the filtering process can be inspected rather than treated as an opaque preprocessing step.

The distinction between direct and projected edges is preserved through to the diagnostic stage rather than merged into a single undifferentiated edge set, because the two edge types carry different evidentiary strength: a direct edge is anchored to a report passage that explicitly names a relationship between two companies (for example, a customer, supplier, or partnership relation disclosed in a risk-factor or business-description section), whereas a projected edge is inferred from co-occurrence at a shared concept node and can range from an economically meaningful link to a spurious link created by two companies mentioning a common generic term in unrelated contexts. Section~III-D defines the diagnostics, including high-degree-concept and manual edge-precision checks, used to evaluate whether this distinction holds in practice.

Let $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{E})$ denote the financial KG, where $\mathcal{V}$ contains company and concept entities, $\mathcal{R}$ contains semantic relationship types, and $\mathcal{E}$ contains triples of the form $(h,r,t)$. PyKEEN \cite{ali2021pykeen} is used to learn company embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{30 \times d}, \quad d=128,
\end{equation}
where each row corresponds to a Dow 30 company. PyKEEN is configured with ComplEx \cite{trouillon2016complex}, which represents each entity as a 64-dimensional complex-valued embedding; the real and imaginary components are concatenated at export time, giving the 128-dimensional real-valued $\mathbf{E}_D$ used by the attention encoder. In addition to the numeric embedding dimensions, the exported embedding artifact contains ticker and entity-alignment metadata so that market observations and KG entities can be joined consistently.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph Quality Evaluation}
Because the downstream RL model depends on the quality of the GraphRAG-derived KG, this study evaluates the graph before interpreting trading performance. The KG evaluation focuses on whether the graph contains meaningful company-level relational structure rather than merely reflecting generic financial-report boilerplate.

Table~\ref{tab:kgdiagnostics} summarizes the diagnostics used. Relation type diversity is measured by the number and distribution of semantic relationship types after filtering, since a useful KG should contain more than one relation type and should not be dominated by a single generic relation such as \texttt{related}. Direct and projected company-company edges are counted separately, as they carry different evidentiary strength (Section~III-C). The filtering ratio compares retained triples with rejected triples (generic metric rows, boilerplate concepts, untyped low-signal relations), and high-degree concepts are inspected as candidate stop concepts, since a concept connecting to too many companies is more likely to reflect general disclosure language than genuine shared structure. Embedding-level diagnostics compare same-sector and cross-sector company-pair similarity to assess whether the embedding space reflects economically interpretable clustering, and a manual edge-precision check samples extracted and projected edges against the source reports to estimate hallucination and support. Section~IV-D extends the link-prediction and embedding-agreement diagnostics with explicit statistical controls (a shuffled-graph baseline, sector- and return-correlation permutation tests).

\subsection{Attention-Based State Encoder}
The central model component is an attention state encoder that maps KG embeddings and OHLCV windows into an allocation-oriented state representation. The selected fixed-portfolio embeddings are used as queries:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{E}_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{M \times d}=\mathbb{R}^{5 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E}_{P}$ is the submatrix of $\mathbf{E}_{D}$ corresponding to AAPL, NVDA, AMZN, IBM, and MSFT. The full Dow 30 company embeddings are used as keys:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{E}_{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}=\mathbb{R}^{30 \times 128}.
\end{equation}

For the values, the market context window for each company is flattened and projected into the same latent dimension:
\begin{align}
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t} &\in \mathbb{R}^{N \times (L F)}=\mathbb{R}^{30 \times 150},\\
\mathbf{V}_{t} &= f_{v}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}=\mathbb{R}^{30 \times 128}.
\end{align}
The attention context is computed by multi-head attention \cite{vaswani2017attention} with $h$ heads. For head $j \in \{1,\ldots,h\}$, the query, key, and value matrices are linearly projected into a lower-dimensional subspace of size $d_h = d/h$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Q}_j = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_j^{Q}, \quad \mathbf{K}_j = \mathbf{K}\mathbf{W}_j^{K}, \quad \mathbf{V}_j = \mathbf{V}_t\mathbf{W}_j^{V},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_j^{Q}, \mathbf{W}_j^{K}, \mathbf{W}_j^{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_h}$ are learned projection matrices specific to head $j$. The main configuration uses $h=4$ heads, so $d_h = 32$. Each head computes a scaled dot-product attention over the fixed-portfolio queries against the full Dow~30 key set:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_j = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_j \mathbf{K}_j^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{head}_j = \mathbf{A}_j \mathbf{V}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times d_h}.
\end{equation}
The per-head attention weight matrix $\mathbf{A}_j$ gives, for each of the five fixed-portfolio stocks, a distribution over all 30 Dow companies indicating which companies' KG embeddings and market context contribute most to that stock's state at trading date $t$; this is the quantity exported as \path{attention_weights.npy} (Section~V) and used for interpretability analysis. The $h$ heads are concatenated and linearly recombined to produce the final attention context:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}_{t}=\mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{head}_h)\mathbf{W}^{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{M \times d}=\mathbb{R}^{5 \times 128},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}^{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a learned output projection and $\mathbf{H}_t$ is the RL state consumed by the allocation head. Because $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are derived from the same static KG embedding matrix $\mathbf{E}_D$, and the attention encoder is frozen after pretraining (Section~III-F), the attention weights $\mathbf{A}_j$ do not change from one trading date to the next: they encode a persistent, date-invariant company-level relational structure, while the value matrix $\mathbf{V}_j$ carries the time-varying market information from the rolling OHLCV window. This separation lets the model use the KG as a fixed structural retrieval mechanism rather than as a direct price predictor.

The allocation head converts $\mathbf{H}_t$ into the stock logits $\mathbf{z}_t$ used in Section~III-A. For each stock $i$, its row $\mathbf{h}_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ of $\mathbf{H}_t$ is concatenated with the corresponding scalar entry of the previous executed weight, $w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t-1}$, and passed through a shared row-wise MLP (the same weights applied independently to each of the $M$ stocks):
\begin{equation}
z_{i,t}=\mathrm{MLP}\!\left(\big[\mathbf{h}_{i,t}\,;\,w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t-1}\big]\right).
\end{equation}
Because the MLP is shared across stocks, any two stocks with identical $[\mathbf{h}_{i,t};w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t-1}]$ inputs receive identical logits; in particular, under the uniform-attention ablation of Section~IV-C, all five rows of $\mathbf{H}_t$ are identical, so the previous executed weight is the only remaining input through which the five logits can differ.

The attention output is not concatenated with a separate own-price encoder, and predicted returns are not inserted into the action state. This design forces the allocation head to act on the learned attention context rather than on a direct return forecast.

\subsection{Two-Stage Training}
The model is trained in two stages. In Stage 1, the attention state encoder is pretrained using the fixed five stocks' next-day returns as a directional supervision signal, so that the attention representation captures market-context information relevant to the selected portfolio rather than serving as a standalone return predictor. The allocation head is also warm-started from the same target by matching a softmax distribution over next-day returns, giving the head a directional prior without feeding the predicted return into the action state.

Let $\mathbf{r}_{t+1}^{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ denote the next-day return vector of the fixed portfolio. The pretrained encoder output $\mathbf{H}_t$ is passed to a return-prediction head:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{t+1}^{P}=g(\mathbf{H}_t).
\end{equation}
The return prediction loss is the mean squared error between predicted and realized next-day returns:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ret}}
=
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\left(\hat{r}_{i,t+1}-r_{i,t+1}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
A pretraining target allocation distribution is formed as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\kappa\mathbf{r}_{t+1}^{P}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\kappa>0$ is a return-scaling coefficient controlling the sharpness of the target allocation distribution. The head pretraining loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{head}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{M} y_{i,t+1}\log \hat{w}_{i,t},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}$ is the allocation distribution produced by the warm-started head during pretraining. The total Stage 1 objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pretrain}}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ret}}
+
\eta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{head}},
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ controls the strength of the allocation-head warm-start loss.

In Stage 2, the pretrained attention modules are frozen and only the lightweight allocation head is trained. The return predictor is not fed into the action state, predicted next-day return is not added to the action logits, and turnover is recorded only as a diagnostic metric. Actual transaction cost is handled by the trading environment through the configured commission parameter.

\subsection{Portfolio Policy Optimization and Reward}
The trading environment follows an allocation-rebalancing formulation. The agent outputs target portfolio weights $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_t$ (Section~III-A), and the environment computes the trades required to move from the previous allocation to the current target. Transaction cost is modeled throughout as a multiplicative transaction-remainder factor (TRF) rather than an additive fee, so that the same cost mechanism appears in the environment reward and in the training loss below.

Because one trading day of price variation occurs between when $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_{t-1}$ was set and when the portfolio is rebalanced toward $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_t$, the weights actually held immediately before rebalancing have drifted away from $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_{t-1}$. Let $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{\mathrm{drift}}$ denote this pre-rebalancing weight vector, obtained by applying the previous day's price variation to $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_{t-1}$ and renormalizing:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{w}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{drift}} = \frac{w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t-1}\xi_{i,t}}{\sum_{j=0}^{M} w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{j,t-1}\xi_{j,t}}.
\end{equation}
Given $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{\mathrm{drift}}$, the target weights $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_t$, and a per-trade commission rate $c_{\mathrm{tx}}$, the remainder factor $\mu_t\in(0,1]$ is the fixed point of
\begin{equation}
\mu_t
=
\frac{1-c_{\mathrm{tx}}w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{0,t}-(2c_{\mathrm{tx}}-c_{\mathrm{tx}}^2)\sum_{i=1}^{M}\max\!\big(\tilde{w}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{drift}}-\mu_t w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t},\,0\big)}{1-c_{\mathrm{tx}}w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{0,t}},
\end{equation}
solved by fixed-point iteration at each trading step. The portfolio value then evolves with the next-day price variation:
\begin{equation}
V_{t+1}=\mu_t V_t \sum_{i=0}^{M} w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t}\xi_{i,t+1},
\end{equation}
where $\xi_{i,t+1}$ is the price variation factor of asset $i$ from date $t$ to $t+1$, with $\xi_{0,t+1}=1$ for cash. When cash is disabled, the cash weight is set to zero, $w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{0,t}=0$, and the allocation is normalized over the $M$ stock assets. This study uses log portfolio growth as the environment reward:
\begin{equation}
R_t=\log\left(\frac{V_{t+1}}{V_t}\right).
\end{equation}

The allocation head is optimized by direct gradient ascent on this same realized portfolio growth rather than by a value-based RL algorithm such as DQN or an actor-critic algorithm such as PPO: each mini-batch replays stored market contexts and price variations through the current policy and backpropagates directly through the resulting growth, with no sampled trajectories, value function, or advantage estimate involved. Each mini-batch consists of $B$ consecutive trading days drawn from a single forward pass through the episode, without shuffling, and is flushed once $B$ steps have accumulated. The previous-day weights $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_{b-1}$ that enter $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{b}^{\mathrm{drift}}$ are treated as detached conditioning inputs rather than differentiated quantities, so gradients from step $b$ do not propagate back into earlier trading days through this feedback path. For a mini-batch of $B$ trading steps, let $\xi_{i,b}$ denote the price variation factor of asset $i$ at batch element $b$, with $\xi_{0,b}=1$ for cash, and let $\mu_b$ denote the same transaction-remainder factor defined above, computed from $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{b}^{\mathrm{drift}}$ and $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{exec}}_b$ at batch element $b$. The realized one-step portfolio growth is
\begin{equation}
G_b
=
\mu_b \sum_{i=0}^{M} w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,b}\xi_{i,b}.
\end{equation}
The RL-stage optimization loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RL}}
=
-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log(G_b).
\end{equation}
Thus, maximizing portfolio growth is equivalent to minimizing negative log realized growth.

Entropy, turnover, concentration-related quantities, and stock-weight dispersion are recorded as diagnostics rather than included as reward terms, so that low turnover or diversified weights are attributed only to the learned policy and the commission-based transaction cost, not to an explicit turnover penalty or entropy reward. Turnover compares consecutive executable target weights rather than the pre-rebalancing drifted weight:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Turnover}_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M}\left|w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t}-w^{\mathrm{exec}}_{i,t-1}\right|.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Computational Cost and Inference Latency}
The proposed framework separates computation into an offline stage and an online stage. The offline stage covers GraphRAG indexing and PyKEEN embedding training (Section~III-C), which runs once per KG refresh cycle over a static corpus of financial reports to produce $\mathbf{E}_D$, and attention-encoder pretraining (Stage~1 of Section~III-F), which runs once before any policy optimization. Neither step is on the trading decision path. The online stage is the path actually exercised at trading time: given the frozen encoder and the precomputed $\mathbf{E}_D$ and $\mathbf{E}_P$, computing $\mathbf{H}_t$ from a rolling OHLCV window is a fixed-size forward pass through multi-head attention and the allocation head, with no LLM call and no GraphRAG query in the loop. This offline/online separation is a direct consequence of the freezing design in Section~III-F, and it confines the expensive LLM-based components to the KG-construction stage rather than the per-decision inference path.

\subsection{Main Comparison and Ablations}
The experimental protocol is organized into a main comparison, external benchmarks, and a set of ablations that isolate specific architectural and data choices.

\textbf{Main comparison.} The core comparison consists of three allocation strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Equal weight}: a non-learning benchmark that allocates the five selected stocks equally.
    \item \textbf{Uniform-attention ablation}: the financial-report KG embeddings are replaced by a single shared vector repeated across all 30 Dow companies. This is realized by an \path{--embedding-source identical} flag on \path{run_attention_fixed5.py}, which sets every row of $\mathbf{E}_D$ to the same vector, while leaving the OHLCV-derived value matrix $\mathbf{V}_t$ and the rest of the architecture and training procedure unchanged. Because $\mathbf{Q}$ is itself a submatrix of $\mathbf{E}_D$, both the query and key rows become identical, so the pre-softmax attention scores $\mathbf{Q}_j\mathbf{K}_j^{\top}$ collapse to the same value for every (query, company) pair: the attention weights $\mathbf{A}_j$ become exactly uniform \emph{and} identical across all five query rows, so the five rows of $\mathbf{H}_t$ reduce to the same unweighted average of the 30 companies' OHLCV value vectors. The value pathway, downstream allocation head, and nominal parameter count are retained, but the effective company-specific routing capacity of the query--key pathway is removed; the only remaining source of differentiation between the five stocks' allocation scores is the previous-executed-weight feature described in Section~III-E. This variant is therefore an ablation of non-uniform KG-conditioned aggregation rather than a complete price-only baseline. The identical embedding input is deterministic, although the subsequent encoder pretraining and policy optimization remain stochastic.
    \item \textbf{KG-attention RL}: the proposed model that combines GraphRAG-derived PyKEEN company embeddings with OHLCV value vectors through the attention state encoder.
\end{enumerate}
The equal-weight benchmark evaluates whether the learned strategies outperform a simple diversification rule. The uniform-attention ablation evaluates whether non-uniform company aggregation contributes information beyond an unweighted market-context representation. The KG-attention RL model evaluates whether GraphRAG-derived relational embeddings provide a useful basis for that non-uniform aggregation.

\textbf{External benchmarks.} The following reference strategies are evaluated alongside the three core allocation variants:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[4)] \textbf{MVO}: a mean-variance optimization baseline commonly used in portfolio allocation and FinRL-style backtesting \cite{liu2021finrl}.
    \item[5)] \textbf{DJIA Index}: a market-level benchmark used as an external reference for overall market performance.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Ablations.} Each ablation isolates one architectural or data-source choice while holding the rest of the pipeline fixed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[6)] \textbf{Frozen-versus-unfrozen encoder}: tests whether separating representation learning from policy optimization (Section~III-F) improves training stability.
    \item[7)] \textbf{Cash-enabled variant}: tests whether adding a cash asset reduces drawdown or merely suppresses equity exposure.
    \item[8)] \textbf{GCN-on-price-correlation RL}: replaces the KG-derived company embeddings with node embeddings learned by a graph convolutional network over a price-correlation graph among the Dow~30 companies, while retaining the remaining attention state-encoder architecture. This comparison is designed to test whether any observed benefit is specific to financial-report-derived semantic structure or can also be obtained from a graph constructed solely from historical price relationships.
    \item[9)] \textbf{Numerical-only FinRL baseline}: a standard FinRL-style agent trained on OHLCV and technical-indicator features without any attention state encoder or company-level relational structure, as a reference point independent of the attention architecture in Section~III-E.
    \item[10)] \textbf{KG perturbation stress test}: injects controlled corruption into the ticker graph before PyKEEN training, at increasing corruption rates, in three forms: (i) edge removal, dropping a fraction of true triples; (ii) relation shuffling, reassigning a fraction of triples to a randomly chosen relation type while keeping the head/tail entities fixed; and (iii) false-edge injection, adding fabricated company-company or company-concept triples not supported by any report. For each corruption rate, the full two-stage pipeline (Sections~III-C--III-F) is rerun and both the KG validation metrics (Section~IV-D) and the downstream allocation metrics (Table~\ref{tab:results}) are recorded, so that a degradation curve can be plotted against corruption rate. This directly probes how sensitive the trading strategy is to the kind of extraction errors discussed in Section~II-C, rather than assuming the pipeline's filtering and diagnostics fully eliminate that risk.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{KG Validation Suite}
Because the KG is constructed by an LLM-based extraction pipeline that is not guaranteed to be error-free (Section~II-C), and because RL backtest performance alone is too noisy a signal to certify KG usefulness on its own, the experimental design includes an RL-independent validation suite for evaluating KG quality before interpreting trading results. The suite is organized at three levels.

The first level repeats the graph-statistics diagnostics of Table~\ref{tab:kgdiagnostics} (relation-type diversity, triple accept/reject rate, direct-versus-projected edge counts) directly against the snapshot's stored triples, so that these numbers are reproducible from the database record rather than only from a one-off script run.

The second level evaluates the PyKEEN embeddings as link predictors. Held-out mean reciprocal rank (MRR) and Hits@$k$ are recorded from the PyKEEN training run, and compared against a \emph{shuffled-graph control}: the same triple set with tail entities permuted, retrained under an identical PyKEEN configuration. Because the shuffled graph preserves node degree but destroys true relational structure, its link-prediction metrics estimate the score a KG of this size and density would achieve by chance; the real KG's metrics are only treated as evidence of structure if they sit clearly above this control.

The third level tests whether the embedding space agrees with independently known financial structure, rather than only being internally consistent. A sector-permutation test compares same-sector versus cross-sector cosine similarity among Dow~30 company embeddings against a null distribution obtained by permuting sector labels, yielding a $p$-value for whether GICS-sector clustering in the embedding space is stronger than chance. A Mantel test compares the company-embedding similarity matrix against the empirical daily-return correlation matrix over the training period, testing whether financial-report-derived structure is associated with realized co-movement without assuming a specific functional form. Finally, a manually curated and human-verified set of known company relationships (\path{data/known_company_pairs.csv}) is used to compute similarity-based and projected-edge-based recall: what fraction of relationships known to hold in reality are actually recovered by the KG, either as high-similarity embedding neighbors or as projected graph edges. Draft, unverified pairs can be included for exploratory numbers, but recall is only reported as a formal result once the corresponding rows have been human-reviewed.

Complementing this RL-independent suite, a lower-variance downstream check compares the KG embeddings with the identical-embedding ablation of Section~IV-C (\path{--embedding-source identical}) at the level of Stage~1 supervised pretraining. For each embedding source and random seed, the frozen encoder is evaluated using the evaluation-period information coefficient, defined as the Spearman correlation between predicted and realized next-day returns for the fixed five stocks. The paired per-seed comparison is analyzed with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test when the number of completed seeds is sufficient. Because this supervised objective generally has lower optimization variance than the full sequential policy-training stage, it provides a more direct comparison of the embedding inputs. Both embedding sources are subsequently evaluated through the complete two-stage pipeline to determine whether any Stage~1 advantage persists in portfolio performance (Table~\ref{tab:results}).

Together, these checks are designed so that a KG-attention RL result is not interpreted in isolation: an improvement in Table~\ref{tab:results} is more credible if it is accompanied by link-prediction metrics that clear the shuffled-graph control, sector and return-correlation structure that clears its respective permutation test, non-trivial known-pairs recall, and a Stage~1 information-coefficient advantage over the identical-embedding control, than if it is reported without this supporting evidence.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Random Seeds}
Because the GraphRAG extraction stage depends on a choice of underlying LLM, and provider-hosted LLMs are periodically updated, an unversioned dependency on ``the LLM'' would make the GraphRAG output, and therefore the downstream KG diagnostics and trading results, difficult to reproduce exactly. The experimental protocol therefore records the specific LLM identifier and API version used for entity and relation extraction, the prompt template version, the sampling parameters, and the extraction date, archived alongside the run configuration described in Section~V. Where multiple LLM choices are evaluated, for example a larger proprietary model against a smaller open-weight extraction model, the KG diagnostics in Table~\ref{tab:kgdiagnostics} and the manual edge-precision protocol are used as the basis for comparison. Extraction cost can be recorded separately through the number of processed documents, LLM calls, and tokens, but these quantities are not part of the online inference path described in Section~III-H.

All stochastic components other than PyKEEN training — including attention-encoder pretraining, allocation-head optimization, and the embedding-source ablation of Section~IV-C — are evaluated using the same predefined seed set, since single-seed results could overstate or understate the actual benefit of the KG-attention state. The identical embedding input used by the uniform-attention ablation is itself deterministic, but its downstream neural-network training is repeated across the same seeds as the KG-attention model. Portfolio and representation-learning metrics are reported as the mean and standard deviation across completed seeds, together with individual seed results where space permits.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The evaluation uses both return and risk metrics. Let $V_t$ denote portfolio value. The cumulative return over the evaluation period is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CR}=\frac{V_T}{V_0}-1.
\end{equation}
The annualized return is computed as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AR}=\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{\frac{252}{T}}-1.
\end{equation}
The annualized volatility is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AV}=\sqrt{252}\cdot \mathrm{Std}(r_t).
\end{equation}
The Sharpe ratio is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SR}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[r_t-r_f]}{\mathrm{Std}(r_t-r_f)}\sqrt{252}.
\end{equation}
The maximum drawdown is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MDD}=\max_t\left(\frac{\max_{\tau\leq t}V_{\tau}-V_t}{\max_{\tau\leq t}V_{\tau}}\right).
\end{equation}

\section{Implementation Details}
The main experiment entry point is \path{run_attention_fixed5.py}. The required artifacts are \path{data/price_ohlcv.csv} and \path{artifacts/pykeen/pykeen_entity_embeddings.csv}. The embedding file is expected to contain 30 rows, 128 numeric embedding columns, and ticker/entity alignment metadata.

Each run writes a timestamped directory containing the learned policy, run configuration, summary metrics, equal-weight baseline outputs, training diagnostics, and test outputs. The attention context is stored as \path{attention_context.npy} with shape $\mathrm{dates} \times 5 \times 128$, while attention weights are stored as \path{attention_weights.npy} with shape $\mathrm{dates} \times \mathrm{heads} \times 5 \times 30$. These outputs support both performance evaluation and interpretability analysis. Because the attention weights are fixed after Stage~1 pretraining (Section~III-E), the per-date entries of \path{attention_weights.npy} reflect the same learned structural pattern rather than a signal that changes with market conditions; the array is indexed by date only for convenient alignment with the corresponding \path{attention_context.npy} entries. In particular, the attention weights can be inspected to identify which Dow 30 companies contribute most strongly to each fixed portfolio stock's state representation under the trained encoder.

The diagnostic pipeline also writes GraphRAG usefulness outputs, including company-concept edges, direct company edges, projected company-directed edges, projected company edges, and stop concepts, which provide the basis for the KG quality analysis in Table~\ref{tab:kgdiagnostics}.

\section{Limitations}
This study should be interpreted as an architecture experiment rather than a fully leakage-free historical backtest. The static KG is built once from the financial reports available at construction time and applied uniformly across the evaluation period; a causal backtest would instead require dated KG snapshots restricted to reports available before each evaluation date, and claims about historical trading performance should be read with this in mind unless the KG construction process is extended to a point-in-time design.

A second limitation concerns market-data model selection. Although PyKEEN uses a validation split of KG triples for embedding training (Table~\ref{tab:pykeen-config}), the portfolio experiment does not reserve a separate chronological validation interval distinct from the evaluation interval (Section~IV-B); this is a distinct notion of ``validation'' from the PyKEEN triple split and should not be conflated with it. The hyperparameters in Table~\ref{tab:config} are therefore tuned on the same market window used for the headline metrics in Table~\ref{tab:results}, rather than through an unbiased out-of-sample step. A stricter protocol would reserve a separate chronological validation interval between the training and evaluation periods, or use walk-forward evaluation across multiple non-overlapping evaluation windows, so that hyperparameter selection and final reporting never share the same period.

Finally, the model is allocation-only: it does not perform stock selection, does not model monthly rebalance offsets, and does not claim to select the best stocks from the Dow 30 or S\&P 500 universe. Its empirical question is narrower: whether a financial-report KG attention state improves daily allocation among a fixed set of five stocks.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents an LLM-augmented knowledge graph framework for reinforcement learning in stock trading, which reformulates the original KG-enhanced DRL idea as a fixed-portfolio allocation problem. It constructs a financial-report KG with GraphRAG, learns PyKEEN company embeddings, uses these embeddings to build an attention state encoder over Dow 30 market context, and freezes the pretrained encoder before portfolio policy optimization. This design connects non-structured financial disclosures to portfolio allocation while limiting the instability of end-to-end representation learning.

Beyond the allocation head, the framework contributes an auditable pipeline for constructing and evaluating a financial-report-derived KG before interpreting downstream trading performance. The evaluation protocol separately examines relation diversity, direct and projected company links, filtering behavior, rejected triples, high-degree concepts, embedding similarity structure, and manually reviewed edge precision. These diagnostics are intended to distinguish economically meaningful company-level structure from generic financial-report noise and to provide supporting evidence for any performance difference observed between the KG-attention model, the uniform-attention ablation, and the external benchmarks.

\subsection{Post-Softmax Cap Operator}
This appendix gives the constructive definition of the cap operator $\Pi_{w_{\max}}$ referenced in Section~III-A and shows why it satisfies $\Pi_{w_{\max}}(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t)_i\in[0,w_{\max}]$ and $\sum_i \Pi_{w_{\max}}(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t)_i=1$.

Given $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t$ summing to 1 over the $M$ stocks and a cap $w_{\max}\in[1/M,1)$, the capped weights are obtained by iterative water-filling: let $\mathbf{w}^{(0)}=\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t$, and at iteration $k$,
\begin{equation}
c_i^{(k)}=\min\!\big(w_i^{(k)},w_{\max}\big), \quad
\epsilon^{(k)}=\sum_i \max\!\big(w_i^{(k)}-w_{\max},0\big),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
r_i^{(k)}=\max\!\big(w_{\max}-c_i^{(k)},0\big), \quad
w_i^{(k+1)}=c_i^{(k)}+\epsilon^{(k)}\frac{r_i^{(k)}}{\sum_j r_j^{(k)}},
\end{equation}
repeated until $\epsilon^{(k)}=0$; the result is $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{cap}}_t=\mathbf{w}^{(k^\ast)}$.

\textbf{Convergence.} An entry once clipped to $w_{\max}$ has $r_i=0$ and therefore never receives further reallocated mass in any later iteration, so the set of capped entries is monotonically non-decreasing across iterations. Since there are only $M$ entries, $\epsilon^{(k)}=0$ is reached within at most $M$ iterations.

\textbf{Mass conservation.} Clipping removes exactly $\epsilon^{(k)}$ from the over-cap entries, and the same $\epsilon^{(k)}$ is fully redistributed to entries with $r_i^{(k)}>0$ (since $\sum_i \epsilon^{(k)} r_i^{(k)}/\sum_j r_j^{(k)} = \epsilon^{(k)}$), so $\sum_i w_i^{(k+1)}=\sum_i w_i^{(k)}$ at every iteration. Starting from $\sum_i w_i^{(0)}=1$, this gives $\sum_i w_i^{\mathrm{cap}}=1$ exactly, so the terminal normalization by $\sum_i w_i$ used in the implementation is a floating-point safeguard rather than a substantive rescaling.

This is the property that a naive clip-then-rescale operator lacks: simply clipping entries above $w_{\max}$ and then dividing every entry by the new (sub-1) sum would inflate \emph{all} entries proportionally, including ones already sitting exactly at $w_{\max}$, pushing them back above the cap. The water-filling operator instead sends the excess $\epsilon^{(k)}$ only to entries with strictly positive room $r_i^{(k)}$, so already-capped entries ($r_i=0$) never receive additional mass, and $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{cap}}_t\in[0,w_{\max}]^M$ holds by construction at termination.

\textbf{Scope.} The derivation above assumes the $M$ stock weights sum to 1, which holds in the main no-cash configuration. In the cash-enabled ablation (Section~IV-C, item 7), the cap is instead applied to the stock sub-vector rescaled by its share of non-cash allocation, $\mathbf{w}_t^{\mathrm{stock}}/\sum_i w_{i,t}^{\mathrm{stock}}$, with the result scaled back by that same share; the cash weight itself is never capped and absorbs no redistributed mass. This related but distinct operator satisfies the same two properties on the stock sub-vector, but $\Pi_{w_{\max}}$ as defined above applies only to the no-cash case.
